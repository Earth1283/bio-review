<Simple>

This unit is about how a population grows when life is perfect: endless food, endless space, and no problems getting worse as it grows. Basically an all-you-can-eat buffet that never closes. Real places are never like this for long, but it is a good place to start.

</Simple>

The exponential model describes how a population grows in an **idealized, unlimited environment**: plenty of food and space, and no disease or predators getting worse as the population gets bigger. No real environment is like that for long, but the model is the baseline every other model is built on.

## Change in size is births minus deaths

<Simple>

A population gets bigger when more are born than die. It gets smaller when more die than are born. That's it. That is the whole idea.

The letter **r** tells you how fast each individual adds to the population. If r is more than 0, the population grows. If r is exactly 0, it stays the same size. If r is less than 0, it gets smaller.

</Simple>

Populations change through births, deaths, immigration, and emigration. To keep the model simple, ignore migration. Then the change in population size $N$ over a stretch of time $t$ is:

$$
\frac{\Delta N}{\Delta t} = B - D
$$

<Aside>
**Per capita** means per individual. If 100 rabbits produce 34 offspring in a year, the per capita birth rate is 0.34.
</Aside>

Births and deaths both scale with how many individuals there are. Write them as per capita rates: $B = bN$ and $D = mN$, where $b$ is the per capita birth rate and $m$ the per capita death rate. Their difference is the **per capita rate of increase**, $r = b - m$.

$$
\frac{\Delta N}{\Delta t} = rN
$$

- If $r > 0$, the population grows.
- If $r = 0$, births and deaths cancel out. This is **zero population growth** (ZPG). Births and deaths still happen; they just balance.
- If $r < 0$, the population shrinks.

## The exponential growth equation

<Simple>

The equation says: **how fast the population grows = r × how many there are now**.

More individuals means faster growth, because there are more parents. Two rabbits make a few rabbits. A thousand rabbits make a LOT of rabbits.

</Simple>

Ecologists usually write the same idea for an instant in time rather than a stretch of it. Under ideal conditions the per capita rate is at its maximum, $r_{\text{max}}$, the **intrinsic rate of increase**:

$$
\frac{dN}{dt} = r_{\text{max}}N
$$

<NoCalculus>

$\dfrac{dN}{dt}$ just means **how fast $N$ is changing right now**. It's the same thing as $\dfrac{\Delta N}{\Delta t}$ above, measured over a moment so short it's practically zero.

You can get the same behavior without calculus by counting one year at a time: each year, add $r$ times the current population.

| Year | Population | Added this year ($r = 0.5$) |
| ---- | ---------- | --------------------------- |
| 0    | 100        | 50                          |
| 1    | 150        | 75                          |
| 2    | 225        | 112.5                       |
| 3    | 337.5      | 168.75                      |
| 4    | 506.25     | 253.1                       |

The amount added grows every year, because it's always a fixed fraction of a bigger and bigger population. That is all exponential growth is. The calculus version does the same thing in infinitely small steps, so you get a smooth curve instead of a staircase.

On a test, this equation is a multiplication problem. If $N = 2{,}000$ and $r_{\text{max}} = 0.05$ per year, then the population is growing by $0.05 \times 2{,}000 = 100$ individuals per year.

</NoCalculus>

## The J-shaped curve

<Simple>

Draw exponential growth on a graph and it looks like the letter **J**. At first it is slow and boring. Then it goes faster, and faster, and then it shoots straight up.

This happens because every new baby will later have babies too. It is like a rumor at school: one person tells two people, they each tell two more, and by lunch everyone knows.

</Simple>

Plotted over time, exponential growth makes a **J-shaped curve**. It starts out looking flat, then shoots upward. The rate $r$ never changes. What changes is $N$: the more individuals there are, the more new ones get added each unit of time.

<Aside>
**Doubling time** is about $0.69 / r$. With $r = 0.35$ per year, the population doubles every 2 years.
</Aside>

Move the time slider and watch the right-hand chart. For exponential growth, the number of new individuals per unit time rises in a straight line with $N$. A population of 400 adds twice as many individuals as a population of 200.

A larger $r_{\text{max}}$ makes the curve steeper. Bacteria and insects can have very high intrinsic rates of increase; large mammals have low ones. Given enough time, though, every exponentially growing population ends up with a J-shaped curve.

## Where you see it in nature

<Simple>

This kind of growth happens when a population arrives in a new, empty place, or when it bounces back after something wiped most of it out.

It never lasts forever. Sooner or later the buffet runs out of food.

</Simple>

Exponential growth shows up when a population is far below the limits of its environment, typically:

- when it **colonizes a new environment**, such as an invasive species arriving somewhere with no competitors, or
- when it **rebounds after a catastrophe** that removed most of it, such as hunting or a fire.

A classic example is the African elephants of Kruger National Park in South Africa. After hunting was banned there, the population grew close to exponentially for about 60 years, at roughly 4% per year, until it was large enough to damage its own habitat.

No population can keep this up. Resources run out, and growth slows. That is the next unit.
