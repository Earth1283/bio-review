The same ideas from the earlier units apply to us: growth rates, age structure, carrying capacity. The human population is unusual for how fast it has grown and how quickly that growth is now changing.

## Growth through history

For most of human history the population grew very slowly. Around 1650 there were about 500 million people. Then improvements in agriculture, sanitation, and medicine cut death rates, and growth took off.

<Aside>
Under exponential growth with a constant $r$, the time to add each new billion would keep shrinking. Here it stopped shrinking around 1974.
</Aside>

Look at the last column. Until the 1970s, each new billion arrived faster than the one before, which is what exponential growth looks like. Since then each billion has taken about 12 years. That's not exponential any more. It's closer to adding the same number of people each year.

## No longer exponential, but still increasing extremely rapidly

Remember that exponential growth means a **constant** per capita rate $r$. The human growth rate is not constant:

- It peaked at a little over 2% per year in the 1960s.
- It has fallen since then, to a little under 1% per year today.

So the human population is **no longer growing exponentially**. But 1% of 8 billion is still a huge number. The world adds roughly **70 million people per year**, which is about the population of France or Thailand every year. That's why the population is **still increasing extremely rapidly** in absolute numbers. The UN's 2024 projections have it peaking at around 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s.

## Regional patterns: the demographic transition

<Aside>
**Replacement fertility** is about 2.1 children per woman: two to replace the parents, plus a little extra because some children die before they themselves have children. The world average today is about 2.3.
</Aside>

Growth rates vary enormously between countries. Many countries have gone through the **demographic transition**, a shift from high birth and death rates to low birth and death rates:

1. **High birth rates, high death rates.** The population grows slowly.
2. **Death rates fall** with better food, clean water, sanitation, and medicine, while birth rates stay high. The population grows fast.
3. **Birth rates fall**, especially as women gain access to education, jobs, and contraception. Growth slows.
4. **Low birth rates, low death rates.** The population is stable, or shrinking.

Most industrialized nations are in the last stage. Many countries in sub-Saharan Africa are still in the fast-growing middle stages, and that's where most future growth will happen.

## Age structure

The **age structure** of a population is the relative number of individuals of each age. It's usually drawn as a pyramid, with males on one side and females on the other.

Age structure predicts the future. A wide base means a large generation is about to reach reproductive age. Even if each of those people has only two children, there are so many of them that the population keeps growing for decades. This is called **population momentum**.

Age structure also shapes social needs: schools and jobs for a young population, health care and pensions for an older one.

### Infant mortality and life expectancy

**Infant mortality** (the number of infant deaths per 1,000 live births) and **life expectancy at birth** differ hugely between countries. Both reflect the quality of life children are born into, and they tend to move together: where infant mortality is high, life expectancy is low.

## Global carrying capacity

<Aside>
An **ecological footprint** is the total area of land and water needed to produce everything a person or nation uses and absorb all its waste.
</Aside>

How many people can Earth support? Estimates vary enormously, because the answer depends on how people live. A population that eats less meat and uses less energy has a smaller **ecological footprint** per person, so more people fit. People in wealthy countries have footprints several times larger than people in the poorest countries.

Unlike most species, humans can change their own carrying capacity, through agriculture and technology for example. Whether the population levels off through falling birth rates, or through rising death rates as resources run short, is still being decided.
