<Simple>

In real life, food and space run out. When a place gets crowded, each individual gets less, so the population grows more slowly. Think of a party: the first guests have lots of room and snacks. The hundredth guest gets a corner and half a cracker.

</Simple>

Exponential growth assumes resources never run out. Real environments fill up. As a population gets more crowded, each individual gets less food, water, space, and shelter, and the population grows more and more slowly. The logistic model describes this.

## Carrying capacity

<Simple>

**Carrying capacity**, written **K**, is the biggest population a place can feed and hold for a long time.

It can change. A rainy year with lots of grass can raise it. A dry year can lower it. K is not a rule the animals agreed to. It is just what the place can handle.

</Simple>

<Aside>
**Limiting resources** set $K$: energy, water, nutrients, shelter, nesting sites, and refuges from predators.
</Aside>

The **carrying capacity**, $K$, is the maximum population size that a particular environment can sustain. It isn't a fixed number for a species. It varies from place to place, and over time in one place, as the amount of limiting resources changes. A wet year can raise $K$ for a grassland mouse population; a drought lowers it.

## The logistic growth equation

<Simple>

The logistic equation is the exponential equation × **how much room is left**.

When the population is small, there is lots of room, so it grows almost as fast as exponential growth. When it gets close to K, there is almost no room, so growth almost stops. The party is full. No more guests.

</Simple>

The logistic model starts from exponential growth and multiplies it by a term that shrinks as $N$ gets close to $K$:

$$
\frac{dN}{dt} = r_{\text{max}}N\,\frac{K - N}{K}
$$

The fraction $\frac{K-N}{K}$ is the share of the carrying capacity that's still unused:

- When $N$ is tiny compared with $K$, the fraction is close to 1, so growth is close to exponential.
- As $N$ approaches $K$, the fraction approaches 0, and growth slows to a stop.
- If $N$ is above $K$, the fraction is negative, and the population shrinks back toward $K$.

<NoCalculus>

Again, $\dfrac{dN}{dt}$ just means **how many individuals are being added per unit of time, right now**. The right-hand side is plain arithmetic: (growth rate) × (population) × (fraction of room left).

Take $r_{\text{max}} = 1$ per year and $K = 1{,}000$:

| $N$   | Room left, $\frac{K-N}{K}$ | Added per year, $1 \times N \times$ room left |
| ----- | -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| 100   | 0.9                        | 90                                             |
| 250   | 0.75                       | 187.5                                          |
| 500   | 0.5                        | **250** (the most)                             |
| 750   | 0.25                       | 187.5                                          |
| 900   | 0.1                        | 90                                             |
| 1,000 | 0                          | 0                                              |

Small populations add few individuals because there are few parents. Big populations add few because there's no room left. The middle, $N = K/2$, adds the most.

To see the S-curve without calculus, start at some $N$, add the "per year" amount from the table, and repeat. The steps are small at first, biggest in the middle, and small again near the top.

</NoCalculus>

## The S-shaped curve

<Simple>

Draw logistic growth on a graph and it looks like the letter **S**. Slow at the start, fast in the middle, slow again at the top.

It grows fastest when it is at **half of K**. Small populations grow slowly because there are only a few parents. Big populations grow slowly because there is no room. The middle has the best of both.

</Simple>

Plotted over time, logistic growth makes an **S-shaped** (sigmoid) curve: slow at first, then fast, then leveling off at $K$. Scrub through time with the time slider, and watch the dot on the right-hand chart climb and then come back down the hump.

Fisheries use this idea. A fish population held near $K/2$ produces the most new fish each year, so in theory it can sustain the largest catch.

- Raise $r$. The population gets to $K$ sooner, but it doesn't end up any bigger.

## How well does it fit real populations?

<Simple>

Some real populations follow the S shape nicely. Many don't.

Some go above K and then crash back down, because they notice the crowding too late (like eating a whole cake and only then feeling full). And some very small populations struggle because it is hard to find a partner when there is almost nobody around.

</Simple>

Some lab populations follow it closely. *Paramecium* grown in a constant environment with a steady food supply trace out a nearly perfect S-curve. Most populations don't, because the model makes simplifying assumptions:

- **No time lags.** The model assumes a population reacts instantly to crowding. In reality there's a delay. Water fleas (*Daphnia*), for example, overshoot $K$ because they keep reproducing on stored energy, then crash and oscillate before settling down.
- **Every new individual reduces growth by the same amount.** In some populations, individuals do better at moderate density than when very rare. This is the **Allee effect**: a single plant is more exposed to wind than one in a clump, and scattered animals may struggle to find mates. Very small populations can then decline toward extinction even with plenty of resources.
- **$K$ stays constant.** In nature, $K$ shifts with seasons, weather, and other species.

The logistic model is still useful. It's a starting point for asking why a real population behaves differently, and it guides conservation work, such as estimating how quickly an endangered population could recover.
