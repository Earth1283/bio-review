<Simple>

A **population** is a group of the same kind of living thing in the same place. All the deer in one forest. All the pigeons in one city (yes, even that one pigeon that keeps staring at you).

This unit is about three things: how many there are in a space (**density**), how they are spread out (**dispersion**), and who is born and who dies (**demographics**). Other living things change all three. These are **biotic** factors, like food, predators, and germs. Non-living things change them too. These are **abiotic** factors, like heat, water, and sunlight.

</Simple>

A **population** is a group of individuals of one species living in the same general area at the same time. Members of a population rely on the same resources, are affected by the same environmental factors, and are likely to interact and breed with one another.

This unit covers three ways of describing a population: how crowded it is (**density**), how it's spread out (**dispersion**), and who is being born and dying (**demographics**). All three are shaped by **biotic** factors (living things: food, predators, competitors, disease, mates) and **abiotic** factors (non-living conditions: temperature, water, light, soil, nutrients, disturbances such as fire).

## Density: how many per unit of space

<Simple>

**Density** means how crowded a population is: how many there are in one area.

Scientists almost never count every single animal. Fish do not line up to be counted. So scientists count a small part and multiply. **Mark–recapture** is one way to do it. Catch some animals, put a harmless mark on them, and let them go. Later, catch some again. If lots of the new catch have marks, the population is small. If almost none have marks, the population is big.

</Simple>

<Aside>
**Density** is the number of individuals per unit area or volume, such as 120 oak trees per km² or 5 million bacteria per mL.
</Aside>

You can rarely count every individual, so ecologists sample and scale up.

- **Quadrats**: count individuals in several randomly placed plots, find the average per plot, and multiply up to the whole area. This works best for things that don't move, such as plants and barnacles.
- **Indirect counts**: count signs of the organism, such as nests, burrows, tracks, or droppings.
- **Mark–recapture**: for animals that move around. Try it below.

### Mark–recapture

Catch $s$ animals, mark them, and release them. Once they've mixed back in, catch a second sample of $n$ animals and count how many of them, $x$, are marked. If the marked animals make up the same fraction of the second catch as they do of the whole population:

$$
\frac{x}{n} = \frac{s}{N}
$$

Rearranged to solve for $N$:

$$
N = \frac{s \times n}{x}
$$

Use "Catch again" several times on the same pond. Each estimate is a little different, and bigger catches give estimates that land closer to the true number. The method only works if:

- marked and unmarked animals are equally likely to be caught (a trap-shy marked animal makes the estimate too high),
- the marked animals mix back into the population completely, and
- there are no births, deaths, immigration, or emigration between the two catches.

### What changes density

<Aside>
Ecologists write this as $\Delta N = B + I - D - E$ over some stretch of time.
</Aside>

Density goes **up** with **births** and **immigration** (individuals arriving from elsewhere), and goes **down** with **deaths** and **emigration** (individuals leaving). Each of these four is pushed around by biotic and abiotic factors. A drought raises deaths; a new food source can raise births and draw in immigrants.

## Dispersion: the spacing pattern

<Simple>

**Dispersion** is the pattern of where individuals are.

- **Clumped**: they stay in groups, like friends sitting together at lunch.
- **Uniform**: they are evenly spaced, like people at a bus stop who really don't want to talk to each other.
- **Random**: there is no pattern at all.

The pattern tells you why. Animals that guard their own space end up evenly spread out. Animals that need the same rare pond all end up clumped around it.

</Simple>

**Dispersion** is the pattern of spacing among individuals within the population's boundaries. It's worth knowing because the pattern tells you something about the environment and about how individuals interact.

## Demographics: births and deaths by age

<Simple>

**Demographics** are numbers about who is born and who dies, and at what age.

A **life table** follows a group born at the same time and counts how many are still alive each year. A **survivorship curve** is the same thing drawn as a picture.

- **Type I**: most live to old age. Humans and elephants.
- **Type II**: the same share dies at every age. Danger is always there, but never extra.
- **Type III**: almost all die as babies, but the few that make it can live a long time. Oysters and most fish. Being a baby oyster is a very tough job.

</Simple>

**Demography** is the study of the vital statistics of a population and how they change over time. Its main tools are life tables and survivorship curves.

### Life tables

A **life table** is an age-by-age summary of survival. The most common kind follows a **cohort**, a group of individuals of the same age, from birth until all of them have died. Here's one for a made-up cohort of 1,000 beetles:

| Age (years) | Alive at start of year | Died during year | Death rate      |
| ----------- | ---------------------- | ---------------- | --------------- |
| 0–1         | 1,000                  | 400              | 400 ÷ 1,000 = 0.40 |
| 1–2         | 600                    | 150              | 150 ÷ 600 = 0.25   |
| 2–3         | 450                    | 225              | 225 ÷ 450 = 0.50   |
| 3–4         | 225                    | 225              | 225 ÷ 225 = 1.00   |

<Aside>
Demographers usually track only **females** in sexually reproducing species, because only females produce offspring. A **reproductive table** (fertility schedule) lists the average number of female offspring per female at each age.
</Aside>

The death rate for an age group is the number that died during that interval divided by the number alive at its start.

### Survivorship curves

A **survivorship curve** plots the number of a cohort still alive at each age. It's normally drawn for 1,000 individuals with a **logarithmic** y-axis, so that a constant death rate shows up as a straight line. Ecologists sort curves into three idealized types:

</div>

Most real species fall somewhere between these types, or combine them. Many birds, for example, have high mortality as chicks (Type III-like) and then a fairly constant death rate as adults (Type II-like).
