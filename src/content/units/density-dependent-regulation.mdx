The logistic model says growth slows as a population gets crowded. This unit is about what actually does the slowing, and why it matters that those factors depend on density.

## Density-dependent and density-independent factors

<Aside>
A factor is density **dependent** if its per capita effect changes with density. A hailstorm that kills 20% of a population whether it's crowded or sparse is density **independent**.
</Aside>

- A **density-independent** factor affects the same *proportion* of a population no matter how dense it is. Most are **abiotic**: frost, drought, fire, floods, storms.
- A **density-dependent** factor gets stronger as density rises: the **death rate goes up** or the **birth rate goes down** in a crowded population. Most are **biotic**.

Only density-dependent factors can **regulate** a population, meaning push it back toward a stable size. That's because they create **negative feedback**: crowding makes things worse for each individual, which slows growth, which eases the crowding.

## How density dependence works

Ecologists have found many density-dependent mechanisms in nature:

| Mechanism                   | How crowding slows growth                                                                                                                  |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Competition for resources** | More individuals share the same food and nutrients, so each gets less, and birth rates fall. Crowded plants produce fewer seeds.           |
| **Territoriality**          | When all territories are taken, extra animals can't breed. Space, rather than food, becomes the limit.                                       |
| **Disease**                 | Pathogens spread faster when hosts are close together, so the death rate rises with density.                                                 |
| **Predation**               | Predators may switch to whichever prey is most abundant, and catch a larger share of a crowded prey population.                              |
| **Toxic wastes**            | Waste builds up in crowded conditions. Yeast fermenting grapes is eventually poisoned by the ethanol it makes.                               |
| **Intrinsic factors**       | Some animals' own physiology changes at high density. Crowded white-footed mice reproduce less even with plenty of food, because of hormonal changes and aggression. |

## Population dynamics

Populations in nature rarely sit still at $K$. **Population dynamics** is the study of how and why population sizes change over time.

On Isle Royale, an island in Lake Superior, ecologists have tracked moose and the wolves that hunt them for decades. Both populations have risen and crashed repeatedly, driven by a mix of density-dependent factors (wolf predation, food supply) and density-independent ones (harsh winters, and disease that swept through the wolves).

### Predator–prey cycles

Some populations rise and fall in regular **cycles**. The best known is the snowshoe hare and the lynx that eats it, in the forests of northern Canada. Both peak roughly every 10 years, with lynx peaks trailing just behind hare peaks. That cycle shows up in more than a century of fur-trapping records.

The simplest model of predators and prey is the **Lotka–Volterra model**. With $N$ prey and $P$ predators:

$$
\frac{dN}{dt} = rN - aNP
$$

$$
\frac{dP}{dt} = baNP - mP
$$

<NoCalculus>

Each equation says **how fast that population is changing right now**. Read it as a sentence:

- **Prey change** = (prey born: $rN$) − (prey eaten: $aNP$).
- **Predator change** = (new predators made from prey eaten: $b \times aNP$) − (predators dying: $mP$).

The prey-eaten term, $aNP$, multiplies the two populations because a predator has to *meet* a prey animal to eat it, and meetings are more common when both are abundant.

You can run the whole model as a story, one step at a time:

1. Lots of prey, few predators: predators feast and multiply.
2. Lots of predators: they eat the prey faster than the prey can reproduce, so prey numbers fall.
3. Few prey left: predators starve and their numbers fall.
4. Few predators: the prey recover. Back to step 1.

The calculus just runs this loop in tiny steps, which is all the simulation below does.

<Aside>
**A surprise from the model:** raise the prey birth rate. The average number of predators goes up, but the average number of prey doesn't change. The extra prey get eaten.
</Aside>

Things to try:

- Look at "Over time". Predator peaks come just after prey peaks, as in the hare and lynx records.
- Switch to "Prey against predators". The cycle traces a loop around a balance point, going counterclockwise.
- Turn on "Prey also limited by food". Now the prey are regulated by density as well, and the swings shrink.

Real hare cycles aren't explained by predators alone. Field experiments in the Yukon added food, kept predators out, or both. Hare populations grew most when they got extra food *and* protection from predators, so both factors drive the cycle.

## Immigration, emigration, and metapopulations

So far we've treated populations as isolated, but individuals move between them. Many species live as a **metapopulation**: a group of local populations linked by dispersal.

The Glanville fritillary butterfly in the Åland Islands of Finland lives in hundreds of small meadow patches. Local populations often go extinct, and new ones are founded as butterflies arrive from neighboring patches. The species persists across the region even though no single patch is safe.
